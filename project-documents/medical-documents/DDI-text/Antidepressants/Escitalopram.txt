Suicidality and antidepressants

In short-term studies, antidepressants increased the risk of suicidal thinking and
behavior in children, adolescents, and young adults (<24 years) taking
antidepressants for major depressive disorders and other psychiatric illnesses

increases in the risk of suicide

Serotonin syndrome

Serotonin syndrome reported with SNRIs and SSRIs, particularly when
coadministered with other serotonergic drugs

Serotonin syndrome symptoms may include mental status changes (eg, agitation,
hallucinations, delirium, and coma), autonomic instability (eg, tachycardia, labile
blood pressure, dizziness, diaphoresis, flushing, hyperthermia), neuromuscular
symptoms (eg, tremor, rigidity, myoclonus, hyperreflexia, incoordination), seizures,
and/or gastrointestinal symptoms (eg, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea); monitor for
emergence of serotonin syndrome

Hyponatremia

Hyponatremia may occur with SSRIs and SNRIs

In many cases, hyponatremia appears to be result of syndrome of inappropriate
antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), and was reversible when discontinued

Cases with serum sodium <110 mmol/L reported

Elderly patients may be at greater risk of developing hyponatremia with SSRIs and
SNRIs; also, patients taking diuretics or are otherwise volume depleted may be at
greater risk

Consider discontinuation if symptomatic hyponatremia occurs and institute
appropriate medical intervention

Signs and symptoms include headache, difficulty concentrating, memory
impairment, confusion, weakness, and unsteadiness, which may lead to falls

Signs and symptoms associated with more severe and/or acute cases include
hallucination, syncope, seizure, coma, respiratory arrest, and death

Pregnancy & Lactation

Pregnancy

Available data from published epidemiologic studies and postmarketing reports have not
established an increased risk of major birth defects or miscarriage

There are risks of persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN) and poor
neonatal adaptation with exposure to selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs)
during pregnancy

There are risks associated with untreated depression in pregnancy

Exposure to SSRIs, particularly in month before delivery, associated with <2-fold
increase in risk of postpartum hemorrhage; bleeding events related to drugs that
interfere with serotonin reuptake have ranged from ecchymosis, hematoma, epistaxis,
and petechiae to life-threatening hemorrhages

Use in the month before delivery may be associated with an increased risk of postpartum
hemorrhage

Pregnancy exposure registry

There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors outcomes in women exposed
to antidepressants during pregnancy

Healthcare providers are encouraged to advise patients to register by calling the
National Pregnancy Registry for Antidepressants at 1-844-405-6185 or at
womensmentalhealth.org/research/pregnancyregistry/antidepressants

Neonates exposed to escitalopram and other SSRIs/SNRIs

Neonates exposed to SSRIs/SNRIs late in third trimester have developed
complications requiring prolonged hospitalization, respiratory support, and tube
feeding

Such complications can arise immediately upon delivery

Reported clinical findings include respiratory distress, cyanosis, apnea, seizures,
temperature instability, feeding difficulty, vomiting, hypoglycemia, hypotonia,
hypertonia, hyperreflexia, tremor, jitteriness, irritability, and constant crying

These features are consistent with toxic effects of SSRIs and SNRIs or, possibly,
drug discontinuation syndrome

Disease-associated maternal risk and/or embryo/fetal risk

Women who discontinue antidepressants are more likely to experience a relapse of
major depression than women who continue antidepressants

This finding is from a prospective longitudinal study of 201 pregnant women with a
history of major depression, who were euthymic and taking antidepressants at the
beginning of pregnancy

Consider risk of untreated depression when discontinuing or changing treatment
with antidepressant medication during pregnancy and postpartum
