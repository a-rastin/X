Schizophrenia [cite: 16]
20 mg PO q12hr with food initially; may be increased every other day PRN; not to exceed 80 mg q12hr [cite: 17]
Periodically assess need for maintenance; clinical trials have documented no added benefit with doses above 20 mg q12hr [cite: 18]

Acute Agitation With Schizophrenia [cite: 19]
IM: 10 mg q2hr or 20 mg q4hr; not to exceed 40 mg/day; use IM for up to 3 days, and switch to PO if continuing past this time [cite: 20]

Bipolar I Disorder [cite: 26]
Acute treatment of manic or mixed episodes; maintenance therapy as adjunct to lithium or valproate [cite: 27]
Acute treatment: 40 mg PO q12hr with food initially; on day 2, may be increased if necessary to 60-80 mg PO q12hr; [cite: 28] adjust dose according to tolerance and efficacy within range of 40-80 mg q12hr [cite: 29]
Maintenance: Continue at same dose at which patient was initially stabilized; periodically reassess need for maintenance therapy [cite: 30]

Dosing Modifications [cite: 31]
Renal impairment: Dose adjustment not necessary with PO administration; caution required with IM administration [cite: 32]
Hepatic impairment: Use caution; drug undergoes extensive hepatic metabolism, which can increase systemic exposure [cite: 33]

Monitor Closely (390) [cite: 469]
(Summary of common mechanisms for closely monitored drugs:)
• Antidiabetic agents (e.g., acarbose, glimepiride, glipizide, glyburide, insulin, metformin): Atypical antipsychotics are associated with hyperglycemia; monitor glucose closely. [cite: 470, 471, 472, 948, 949, 950, 951, 952, 953, 954, 955, 956, 1000, 1001, 1002, 1003, 1004, 1005, 1006, 1014, 1015, 1016, 1030, 1031, 1032, 1163, 1164, 1165]
• Sedatives/Opioids/Antihistamines (e.g., alprazolam, baclofen, codeine, diazepam, diphenhydramine, fentanyl, morphine, zolpidem): Both increase sedation. [cite: 493, 494, 548, 549, 682, 684, 750, 751, 779, 780, 875, 879, 1225, 1226, 1662, 1663]
• Anticholinergics (e.g., atropine, dicyclomine, oxybutynin): Decreased levels of ziprasidone by inhibition of GI absorption; additive anticholinergic effects. [cite: 530, 531, 532, 533, 758, 759, 760, 761, 1282, 1283, 1284, 1285]
• CYP3A4 inhibitors/inducers (e.g., carbamazepine, clarithromycin, ketoconazole, rifampin): Alter ziprasidone levels. [cite: 626, 627, 660, 661, 1049, 1050, 1450, 1451]
• Serotonergic agents (e.g., almotriptan, SSRIs, SNRIs): May enhance serotonergic effects and lead to serotonin syndrome; may increase risk for NMS. [cite: 484, 485, 486, 487, 492]
• Antihypertensives (e.g., amlodipine, clonidine): Additive hypotensive effects. [cite: 501, 502, 503, 671, 672]

Minor (57) [cite: 1671]
Minor interaction drugs generally affect CYP3A4 metabolism (either increasing or decreasing ziprasidone levels slightly) or have mild pharmacodynamic synergism/antagonism (e.g., sedation). Examples include aprepitant, bosentan, dexamethasone, fluconazole, marijuana, nifedipine, verapamil. [cite: 1672, 1673, 1674, 1686, 1687, 1688, 1714, 1715, 1733, 1734, 1735, 1764, 1765, 1766, 1782, 1783, 1825, 1826, 1827]

Cautions [cite: 1885]
Seizure disorders; may cause hypotension, EPS, somnolence, and sensory instability, which could lead to falls and injuries; perform complete fall risk assessments when initiating treatment and recurrently for high-risk patients. [cite: 1886, 1887]
Atypical antipsychotics have been associated with metabolic changes (eg, hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and body weight gain) that may increase cardiovascular/cerebrovascular risk. [cite: 1888]
Hyperglycemia may occur and in some cases may be extreme, resulting in ketoacidosis, hyperosmolar coma, or death; monitor blood glucose of high-risk patients. [cite: 1893, 1894]
Neuroleptic malignant syndrome reported. [cite: 1895]
Tardive dyskinesia, acute dystonic reactions, pseudoparkinsonism, or akathisia may develop acutely or chronically. [cite: 1896]
Discontinue if rash develops without an identified cause. [cite: 1897]
DRESS reported; DRESS is sometimes fatal; discontinue therapy if suspected. [cite: 1898, 1899]
Severe cutaneous adverse reactions, such as Stevens-Johnson syndrome, reported; discontinue therapy if suspected. [cite: 1900, 1901]
Rare cases of priapism reported. [cite: 1901]
May cause orthostatic hypotension. [cite: 1902]
Suicide attempt is inherent in psychotic illness or bipolar disorder, close supervision of high-risk patients should accompany therapy. [cite: 1903]
May elevate prolactin levels; long-standing hyperprolactinemia when associated with hypogonadism may lead to decreased bone density. [cite: 1904]
Leukopenia/neutropenia and agranulocytosis reported; monitor CBC frequently and discontinue drug at first sign of clinically significant WBC decline <1000/µL. [cite: 1905, 1906, 1907]
Associated with esophageal dysmotility and aspiration; use caution in patients at risk of pneumonia. [cite: 1908]
May cause QTc prolongation (torsade de pointes) and sudden death; discontinue therapy in patients with persistent QTc intervals >500 msec; avoid hypokalemia or hypomagnesemia. [cite: 1909, 1910]
Moderate to highly sedative; use caution when required to operate heavy machinery. [cite: 1911]
May cause core body temperature regulation impairment; use caution with heat exposure, strenuous exercise, dehydration, or anticholinergic meds. [cite: 1912]
Correct electrolyte imbalance, especially hypomagnesemia or hypokalemia. [cite: 1913]
Use with caution in hepatic impairment. [cite: 1918]

Serotonin Syndrome [cite: 1919]
• Ziprasidone can precipitate serotonin syndrome, a potentially life-threatening condition; risk is increased with concomitant use of other serotonergic drugs (including SSRIs, SNRIs, triptans, TCAs, opioids, lithium, MAOIs, etc.). [cite: 1920, 1921]
• Symptoms include mental status changes, autonomic instability, neuromuscular symptoms, seizures, and GI symptoms. [cite: 1922]
• Concomitant use with MAOIs is contraindicated. [cite: 1923, 1924, 1925]
• Monitor all patients and discontinue immediately if symptoms occur. [cite: 1926, 1927, 1928]

Hyperprolactinemia [cite: 1929]
• Elevates prolactin levels in humans. [cite: 1930]
• Animal studies showed increased mammary gland neoplasia in mice (not rats). [cite: 1931, 1932]
• One-third of human breast cancers are prolactin-dependent in vitro. [cite: 1933]
• Epidemiologic studies show inconsistent results between hyperprolactinemia and breast cancer. [cite: 1934]

Pregnancy & Lactation [cite: 1935]
Pregnancy [cite: 1936]
Pregnancy exposure registry monitors outcomes. [cite: 1937, 1938, 1943]
Neonates exposed during third trimester are at risk for extrapyramidal and/or withdrawal symptoms; overall epidemiologic data have not established drug-associated risk of major birth defects. [cite: 1944, 1945, 1946]
Untreated schizophrenia or bipolar I disorder has risks including relapse, hospitalization, suicide, and adverse perinatal outcomes (preterm birth). [cite: 1947, 1948, 1949]
Animal studies caused developmental toxicity and teratogenicity. [cite: 1953, 1954, 1955]
Reproductive potential: Reversible reduction in fertility in females due to prolactin increase. [cite: 1956, 1957]

Pharmacology [cite: 1976]
Mechanism of Action [cite: 1977]
Acts as antagonist at dopamine D2 and serotonin type 1 and 2 (5HT1D, 5HT2A) receptors; acts as agonist at serotonin 5HT1A receptor; moderately inhibits reuptake of norepinephrine and serotonin; has alpha-blocking and antihistaminic activity. [cite: 1978, 1979]
