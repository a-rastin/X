Warnings [cite: 905]
Black Box Warnings [cite: 906]
Increased mortality in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis [cite: 907]
• Patients with dementia-related psychosis who are treated with antipsychotic drugs are at an increased risk of death as shown in short-term controlled trials [cite: 908]
• Deaths appeared to be either cardiovascular (eg, heart failure, sudden death) or infectious (eg, pneumonia) in nature [cite: 909]
• Not approved for treatment of patients with dementia-related psychosis [cite: 910]

Contraindications [cite: 911]
Hypersensitivity; anaphylaxis, angioedema, and other hypersensitivity reactions reported [cite: 912]

Cautions [cite: 913]
Not approved for dementia-related psychosis [cite: 914]
• Elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis treated with antipsychotic drugs are at an increased risk of death [cite: 915]
• Causes of death were varied, but most deaths appeared to be either cardiovascular (eg, heart failure, sudden death) or infectious (eg, pneumonia) in nature [cite: 916]
• Additionally, comparative trials observed a higher incidence of stroke and transient ischemic attack, including fatal stroke [cite: 917]

QT Prolongation [cite: 918]
• Associated with QTc prolongation [cite: 919]
• Effect of iloperidone on the QT interval is augmented if coadministered with CYP450 2D6 or 3A4 inhibitors [cite: 920]
• Avoid coadministration with other drugs known to prolong QTc including Class 1A (eg, quinidine, procainamide) or Class III (eg, amiodarone, sotalol) antiarrhythmic medications, antipsychotic medications (eg, chlorpromazine, thioridazine), antibiotics (eg, moxifloxacin), or any other class of medications known to prolong the QTc interval (eg, pentamidine, methadone) [cite: 921]
• Also avoided in patients with a known genetic susceptibility to congenital long QT syndrome or history of cardiac arrhythmias [cite: 922]
• Variables that increase risk of torsade de pointes and/or sudden death in association with drugs that prolong the QTc interval: Bradycardia, Hypokalemia or hypomagnesemia, Coadministration with other drugs that prolong QTc interval, electrolyte disturbances before initiating and periodically thereafter. [cite: 923, 924, 925, 926, 932]
• Avoid in patients with histories of significant cardiovascular illness (eg, QT prolongation, recent acute myocardial infarction, uncompensated heart failure, or cardiac arrhythmia) [cite: 933, 934]
• Discontinue with persistent QTc measurements >500 msec [cite: 935]
• If patients experience symptoms that suggest the occurrence of cardiac arrhythmias (eg, dizziness, palpitations, syncope), initiate further evaluation including cardiac monitoring [cite: 936, 937]

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome [cite: 938]
• A potentially fatal symptom complex sometimes referred to as neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) reported in association with administration of antipsychotic drugs [cite: 939]
• Clinical manifestations include hyperpyrexia, muscle rigidity, altered mental status (including catatonic signs) and evidence of autonomic instability (irregular pulse or blood pressure, tachycardia, diaphoresis, and cardiac dysrhythmia) [cite: 940]
• Additional signs may include elevated creatine phosphokinase, myoglobinuria (rhabdomyolysis), and acute renal failure [cite: 941]
• Diagnostic evaluation of patients with this syndrome is complicated; in arriving at a diagnosis, it is important to identify cases in which the clinical presentation includes both serious medical illness (eg, pneumonia, systemic infection) and untreated or inadequately treated extrapyramidal signs and symptoms (EPS) [cite: 942, 943]
• Other important considerations in the differential diagnosis include central anticholinergic toxicity, heat stroke, drug fever, and primary central nervous system (CNS) pathology [cite: 944]
• Management of NMS includes immediate discontinuation of antipsychotic drugs and other drugs not essential to concurrent therapy, intensive symptomatic treatment and medical monitoring, and treatment of any concomitant serious medical problems for which specific treatments are available [cite: 945]
• There is no general agreement about specific pharmacological treatment regimens for NMS; if a patient requires antipsychotic drug treatment after recovery from NMS, the potential reintroduction of drug therapy should be carefully considered; the patient should be carefully monitored since recurrences of NMS have been reported [cite: 946, 947, 948]

Tardive dyskinesia [cite: 949]
• Tardive dyskinesia (TD) is a syndrome consisting of potentially irreversible, involuntary, dyskinetic movements, which may develop in patients treated with antipsychotic drugs [cite: 950]
• Prevalence of TD appears to be highest among elderly patients, especially elderly women, it is impossible to rely on prevalence estimates to predict, at inception of antipsychotic treatment, which patients are likely to develop the syndrome [cite: 951]
• If TD occurs, consult with a specialist to determine if treatment (eg, valbenazine, deutetrabenazine) is appropriate [cite: 957]

Metabolic changes [cite: 958]
• Atypical antipsychotic drugs associated with metabolic changes (eg, hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, weight gain) that may increase cardiovascular/cerebrovascular risk [cite: 959]
• Assess fasting plasma glucose and lipid profile before or soon after initiating and monitor periodically during long-term treatment [cite: 960]
• Monitor weight at baseline and throughout therapy [cite: 961]

Hyperglycemia and diabetes mellitus [cite: 962]
• Hyperglycemia, in some cases extreme and associated with ketoacidosis or hyperosmolar coma or death, reported; assessment of relationship between atypical antipsychotic use and glucose abnormalities is complicated by possibility of an increased background risk of diabetes mellitus in patients with schizophrenia and increasing incidence of diabetes mellitus in general population [cite: 963, 964]
• Relationship between atypical antipsychotic use and hyperglycemia-related adverse events is not completely understood; however, epidemiological studies suggest an increased risk of hyperglycemia-related adverse events in patients treated with atypical antipsychotics [cite: 965, 966]
• Patients with an established diagnosis of diabetes mellitus who are started on atypical antipsychotics should be monitored regularly for worsening of glucose control; patients with risk factors for diabetes mellitus (eg, obesity, family history of diabetes) who are starting treatment with atypical antipsychotics should undergo fasting blood glucose testing at beginning of treatment and periodically during treatment [cite: 967, 968]
• Any patient treated with atypical antipsychotics should be monitored for symptoms of hyperglycemia including polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and weakness; patients who develop symptoms of hyperglycemia during treatment with atypical antipsychotics should undergo fasting blood glucose testing; in some cases, hyperglycemia has resolved when the atypical antipsychotic was discontinued; however, some patients required continuation of antidiabetic treatment despite discontinuation of the suspect drug [cite: 969, 970, 971, 972]

Orthostatic hypotension and syncope [cite: 973]
• Can induce orthostatic hypotension associated with dizziness, tachycardia, and syncope; rapid titration increases this risk [cite: 974]
• This risk reflects iloperidone's alpha1-adrenergic antagonist properties [cite: 975]
• Caution with known cardiovascular disease (eg, heart failure, history of myocardial infarction, ischemia, or conduction abnormalities), or cerebrovascular disease, or conditions that predispose patient to hypotension (dehydration, hypovolemia, and treatment with antihypertensive medications) [cite: 976, 977]
• May cause somnolence, postural hypotension, motor and sensory instability, which may lead to falls and, consequently, fractures or other injuries [cite: 983]
• For patients with diseases, conditions, or medications that could exacerbate these effects, complete fall risk assessments when initiating antipsychotic treatment and recurrently for patients on long-term antipsychotic therapy [cite: 984]

Leukopenia, neutropenia, and agranulocytosis [cite: 985]
• In clinical trial and postmarketing experience, events of leukopenia/neutropenia reported temporally related to antipsychotic agents [cite: 986]
• Agranulocytosis (including fatal cases) also reported [cite: 987]
• Possible risk factors preexisting low white blood cell count (WBC) and history of drug-induced leukopenia/neutropenia; monitor patients with these risk factors frequently during first few months of therapy [cite: 988, 989]
• Discontinue treatment at first sign of a decline in WBC if other causative factors are not apparent [cite: 990]
• Monitor patients with neutropenia for fever or other symptoms or signs of infection and treated promptly if such symptoms or signs occur [cite: 991]
• Discontinue treatment with severe neutropenia (ANC<1000/mm^3) and monitor WBC until recovery [cite: 992]

Hyperprolactinemia [cite: 993]
• As with other drugs that antagonize dopamine D2 receptors, iloperidone elevates prolactin levels. Hyperprolactinemia may suppress hypothalamic GnRH, resulting in reduced pituitary gonadotropin secretion; this, in turn, may inhibit reproductive function by impairing gonadal steroidogenesis in both female and male patients [cite: 994, 995]
• Galactorrhea, amenorrhea, gynecomastia, and impotence reported with prolactin-elevating compounds [cite: 996]
• Long-standing hyperprolactinemia when associated with hypogonadism may lead to decreased bone density in both female and male patients [cite: 997]
• Tissue culture experiments indicate that approximately one-third of human breast cancers are prolactin-dependent in vitro, a factor of potential importance if the prescription of these drugs is contemplated in a patient with previously detected breast cancer [cite: 998]
• Published epidemiologic studies have shown inconsistent results when exploring the potential association between hyperprolactinemia and breast cancer [cite: 999]
• Neither clinical studies nor epidemiologic studies conducted to date have shown an association between chronic administration of this class of drugs and tumorigenesis in humans; the available evidence is considered too limited to be conclusive at this time [cite: 1000, 1001]

Dysphagia [cite: 1008]
• Esophageal dysmotility and aspiration associated with antipsychotic drug use [cite: 1009]
• Aspiration pneumonia is a common cause of morbidity and mortality in elderly patients [cite: 1010]
• Use cautiously in patients at risk for aspiration pneumonia [cite: 1011]

Priapism [cite: 1012]
• Rare cases of priapism reported in pre-marketing program. Drugs with alpha-adrenergic blocking effects reported to induce priapism [cite: 1013]
• Iloperidone elicits alpha1-adrenergic blocking effects [cite: 1014]
• Severe priapism may require surgical intervention [cite: 1015]

Cognitive and motor impairment [cite: 1016]
• Like other antipsychotics, may cause somnolence and has potential to impair judgment, thinking, or motor skills [cite: 1017]
• Caution patients about operating hazardous machinery, including motor vehicles, until they are reasonably certain that iloperidone does not affect them adversely [cite: 1018]

Intraoperative floppy iris syndrome (IFIS) [cite: 1019]
• IFIS observed during cataract surgery in some patients on or previously treated with alpha-1 adrenergic blockers [cite: 1020]
• This variant of small pupil syndrome is characterized by a flaccid iris that billows in response to intraoperative irrigation currents, progressive intraoperative miosis despite preoperative dilation with standard mydriatic drugs, and potential prolapse of the iris toward the phacoemulsification incisions [cite: 1021]
• The patient's surgeon should be prepared for possible modifications to their surgical technique (eg, utilization of iris hooks, iris dilator rings, or viscoelastic substances) [cite: 1022]
• There does not appear to be a benefit of stopping alpha1 blocker therapy before cataract surgery [cite: 1023]
• Initiating iloperidone in patients for whom cataract or glaucoma surgery is scheduled is not recommended [cite: 1024]

Drug interaction overview [cite: 1025]
• Substrate of CYP2D6 and CYP3A4 [cite: 1026]
• Strong CYP2D6 inhibitors: Modify dose [cite: 1027, 1028]
• Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors: Reduce iloperidone dose by 50% when administered with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors [cite: 1034]
• Strong CYP2D6 and CYP3A4 inhibitors: Modify dose. Reduce iloperidone dose by 50% when administered with strong CYP2D6 and CYP3A4 inhibitors. [cite: 1035, 1036]
• Drugs that prolong QT interval: Avoid. Coadministration with other drugs that prolong the QT interval may have additive effects and increase risk of cardiac arrhythmia. Effect of iloperidone on the QT interval is augmented if coadministered with CYP450 2D6 or 3A4 inhibitors. [cite: 1037, 1038, 1039, 1040]
• Drugs that lower blood pressure: Avoid/modify dose. Concomitant use with medications that lower blood pressure may cause symptomatic hypotension. Avoid coadministration with alpha-adrenergic blocking agents. Adjust medications that affect blood pressure as needed. [cite: 1041, 1042, 1043, 1044]

Pregnancy & Lactation [cite: 1045]
Pregnancy [cite: 1046]
There is a pregnancy exposure registry that monitors pregnancy outcomes in women exposed to this medication during pregnancy; for more information contact the National Pregnancy Registry for Atypical Antipsychotics at 1-866-961-2388 or visit https://womensmentalhealth.org/clinical-and-research-programs/pregnancyregistry/ [cite: 1047, 1048]
Neonates whose mothers are exposed to antipsychotic drugs during third trimester of pregnancy are at risk for extrapyramidal and/or withdrawal symptoms following delivery [cite: 1049]
The limited available in pregnant women are not sufficient to inform a drug-associated risk for major birth defects and miscarriage [cite: 1050]
Extrapyramidal and/or withdrawal symptoms, including agitation, hypertonia, hypotonia, tremor, somnolence, respiratory distress, and feeding disorder have been reported in neonates whose mothers were exposed to antipsychotic drugs during third trimester of pregnancy [cite: 1051]
• This drug was not teratogenic when administered orally to pregnant rats during organogenesis at doses up to 26 times maximum recommended human dose of 24 mg/day on mg/m^2 basis; however, it prolonged the duration of pregnancy and parturition, increased stillbirths, early intrauterine deaths, increased incidence of developmental delays, and decreased post-partum pup survival [cite: 1057, 1058]
• The drug was not teratogenic when administered orally to pregnant rabbits during organogenesis at doses up to 20-times the MRHD on mg/m^2 basis; however, it increased early intrauterine deaths and decreased fetal viability at term at highest dose which was also a maternally toxic dose [cite: 1059, 1060]

Lactation [cite: 1061]
There is no information regarding presence of iloperidone or metabolites in human milk, effects on a breastfed child, or on human milk production; the drug is present in rat milk; because of potential for serious adverse reactions in breastfed infants, advise women not to breastfeed during therapy [cite: 1062, 1063]
The transfer of radioactivity into milk of lactating rats was investigated following a single dose of 5 mg/kg; the concentration of radioactivity in milk at 4 hr post-dose was near 10-fold greater than that in plasma at same time; however, by 24 hours after dosing, concentrations of radioactivity in milk had fallen to values slightly lower than plasma; the metabolic profile in milk was qualitatively similar to that in plasma [cite: 1064, 1065, 1066]

Pregnancy Categories [cite: 1067]
A: Generally acceptable. Controlled studies in pregnant women show no evidence of fetal risk. [cite: 1068]
